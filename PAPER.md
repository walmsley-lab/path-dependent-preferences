## Abstract

Training accuracy cannot distinguish between internal strategies that
make identical predictions on the training distribution. We construct a
synthetic social-choice environment in which this indistinguishability
is exact: choices are generated by a latent utility rule, and a second
predictor based on option wording and scene context is constructed to
agree with the utility rule on every training example while disagreeing
on held-out conflict items. We test whether curriculum order determines
which strategy governs behavior. Fifteen 10.9M-parameter decoder-only
Transformers were trained under three orderings of an identical example
multiset (structure-first, choices-first, interleaved), five paired
initialization seeds per condition, with 21 checkpoints preserved per
run. The preregistered endpoint criterion was not met: fourteen of
fifteen runs finished on the utility side of the conflict diagnostic and
paired curriculum differences had mixed signs. The checkpointed record
identifies two effects invisible at the endpoint. First, all five
choices-first runs show a large mid-training decline in conflict
utility-agreement (mean 0.97 to 0.74) beginning when their curriculum
stops presenting choice examples and recovering to 0.93 when the shared
tail restores them; because this decline tracks in-distribution accuracy
almost exactly, it is consistent with practice recency rather than
strategy change. Second, one structure-first run exhibits a late
transition from 0.98 to 0.21 utility-agreement while maintaining
0.99–1.00 in-distribution accuracy, a dissociation of 0.79 against 0.29
for the next most extreme run. Exploratory post-hoc analyses of two
completed runs find preference-class information that is linearly
recoverable across held-out agents and causally influential under
steering, while ablation and cross-run patching controls do not support
unique layer localization or portable state transfer. We report a
follow-up experiment designed to test whether curriculum composition
predicts post-acquisition strategy transitions under preregistered
conditions.

## 1. Introduction

A model can produce correct predictions for more than one reason. When a
training distribution contains several regularities that imply the same
labels, successful optimization does not identify which regularity a
model has learned. Shortcut learning, spurious correlation, and
underspecification are variants of this problem.

The problem is acute when behavior is read as evidence of a stable
disposition. A system that consistently selects outcomes favorable to
another party may have learned to represent and compare outcomes, or may
have learned surface features that correlate with those outcomes in its
training environment. The two are behaviorally indistinguishable until
the regularities are placed in conflict.

A second question follows. When two models receive identical
information, does the order of presentation affect which predictive
strategy they acquire? Curriculum learning typically asks whether
ordering changes performance or sample efficiency. We ask whether it
changes the strategy supporting otherwise similar performance.

Studying this in a large pretrained model would confound the variables
of interest: its training distribution, ordering, and internal
representations are not fully controllable or observable. We therefore
construct a small environment in which the generating process is known
and two strategies can be made exactly observationally equivalent during
training.

The primary question is: **when identical training examples support two
equally predictive rules, does changing only their order change which
rule governs a Transformer's final behavior?** Because we preserve
checkpoints throughout training, we can also ask a second question that
becomes relevant given the primary result: **does endpoint behavior
describe the developmental path by which a model reached it?**

## 2. Environment

### 2.1 Generating rule

The environment contains ten recurring named agents. Each is assigned a
latent preference parameter λ ∈ {0.2, 0.8}, the weight placed on the
agent's own outcome relative to a partner's. Assignment is per seed and
counterbalanced across name-associated demographic categories.

Each scenario presents two alternatives with integer payoff changes for
the agent and the partner. Utility is U(o) = λ·d_self(o) +
(1−λ)·d_other(o), and the generated label is y = argmax_o U(o).
Utilities are computed in integer fifths so that exact ties can be
constructed for diagnostic use. The parameter λ is never stated in the
text presented to the model.

### 2.2 Constructed observational equivalence

After the utility rule determines the label, the generator assigns verb
phrases to the two alternatives. Phrases belong to semantic classes
(cooperative or self-interested, four surface forms each), and the
mapping from class to chosen alternative is conditioned on scene
("market" or "river"). The corpus therefore contains a second rule,
wording × scene → choice, constructed to predict the same label as the
utility rule on every training example. This constraint is verified
mechanically by the generator's invariant tests.

Either strategy achieves perfect training accuracy. The identification
problem is thus present by construction rather than by accident, and its
severity is known exactly (Figure 1).

### 2.3 Diagnostic distributions

Three held-out evaluations separate the strategies. **Conflict** items
assign wording so that the two rules predict opposite alternatives.
**No-cue** items use neutral verb phrases, leaving the wording rule
undefined. **Cue-only** items use exact utility ties, leaving the
utility rule undefined. A fourth in-distribution set measures ordinary
task competence.

Conflict-set agreement with the utility rule is the primary behavioral
measure. In-distribution accuracy measures competence and cannot
identify strategy.

## 3. Design

### 3.1 Models and training

Each run is a six-layer decoder-only Transformer (hidden dimension 384,
six attention heads, 10,924,032 parameters) with a word-level vocabulary
constructed from its own corpus, trained by next-token prediction. Each
seed's corpus contains 1.2M structure lines (payoff relations, neutral
wording, no preference content) and 1.2M choice lines (the only lines
containing preference evidence and the planted wording correlation),
approximately 89M tokens in total. Lines are packed into 320-token
blocks aligned to curriculum phase boundaries; batch size 64; a single
pass over the corpus, giving 4,459 optimizer steps at a flat learning
rate after warmup. Structure lines are deduplicated by exact string and
choice lines by scenario key across surface templates; rejection counts
are recorded in the corpus manifest.

### 3.2 Curriculum intervention

The independent variable is presentation order. Three curricula contain
the same example multiset and an identical final 10% tail (Figure 2):

- **C1**: structure (45%) → choices (45%) → tail (10%)
- **C2**: choices (45%) → structure (45%) → tail (10%)
- **C3**: interleaved (90%) → tail (10%)

The intervention therefore changes when information is encountered, not
what information is available.

Five seeds are used per condition. Within a seed, all three curricula
begin from identical initial parameters, verified by weight hash, which
removes initialization variance from the paired comparison. Optimizer
moments and RNG state are checkpointed at phase boundaries for planned
transfer experiments. A preflight check refuses to launch any run
violating multiset equality, tail identity, or initialization pairing.
Twenty-one checkpoints are preserved per run, giving 15 runs × 21 =
315 evaluated checkpoints.

### 3.3 Preregistration

Calibration established that the two strategies have different
acquisition dynamics in this environment: the wording rule shows a
partial-memorization phase followed by a sharp transition to near-ceiling
accuracy, while the utility rule rises gradually with a persistent
generalization gap and a lower ceiling. Two earlier calibration gates
failed at chance and were traced to undertrained pilot runs; the corpus
was then rescaled from measured acquisition curves. An initial
directional hypothesis — that structure-first training would favor the
utility strategy — was tested in pilot runs and contradicted. That
failure is recorded in the preregistration, which is append-only, and
the main criterion was made direction-agnostic in response.

The preregistered primary criterion required all paired C1−C2 endpoint
differences to share a sign and their mean absolute value to exceed ten
percentage points. This is a preregistered decision rule, not a powered
population-level significance test: requiring five signs to agree is
brittle, and a mean of absolute differences discards direction and is
sensitive to single extreme values. We report it as specified and do not
reuse it in follow-up work (§9).

## 4. Result: no consistent endpoint effect of training order

Conflict-set utility agreement at the final checkpoint:

| Seed | C1 | C2 | C3 | C1 − C2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.783 | 0.815 | 0.783 | −0.033 |
| 1 | 0.990 | 0.940 | 0.955 | +0.050 |
| 2 | 0.978 | 0.953 | 0.735 | +0.025 |
| 3 | 0.215 | 0.960 | 0.993 | −0.745 |
| 4 | 0.980 | 0.983 | 0.993 | −0.003 |

The paired differences have mixed signs, so the preregistered criterion
is not met. The mean absolute difference (0.171) exceeds the threshold
but is produced entirely by seed 3.

Fourteen of fifteen runs finish on the utility side of the conflict
diagnostic (0.735–0.993) under all three orderings. Under the tested
environment, cue complexity, model scale, training budget, and
curriculum manipulation, we find no consistent evidence that training
order determines final strategy preference. The design does not test
equivalence, so this does not establish that curriculum order cannot
affect strategy selection.

Viewed only at the final checkpoint, the single extreme value (C1, seed
3) would reasonably be treated as an anomalous run.

## 5. Result: the checkpointed record

Figure 3 shows conflict utility-agreement across all 21 checkpoints for
all fifteen runs.

### 5.1 A reproducible mid-training effect of ordering

All five C2 runs rise to approximately 0.97 utility-agreement by 50% of
training, decline to a condition mean of 0.74 immediately after 45% —
the point at which the C2 curriculum stops presenting choice examples —
remain at that level throughout the structure phase, and return to a
mean of 0.93 within the shared tail that reintroduces choice examples at
90%. The pattern appears in five of five C2 runs and is absent in C1 and
C3, whose choice examples continue through the corresponding interval.

As a statement about developmental trajectories, curriculum ordering has
a large and fully reproducible effect in this environment. The endpoint
comparison in §4 cannot detect it because C2 recovers before the final
checkpoint.

The most likely explanation is not strategy change. Across the C2
decline, conflict agreement and in-distribution accuracy move together
closely (at 60%: 0.76 and 0.78; at 70%: 0.74 and 0.74; at 95%: 0.94 and
0.95). This co-movement is consistent with degradation of choice-task
competence in the absence of choice examples, and its recovery when they
return, rather than with a transition between strategies.

### 5.2 A late strategy transition in one run

Because degradation and strategy change are confusable at the level of
conflict accuracy alone, we separate them using
D = (in-distribution accuracy − conflict utility-agreement). Degradation
moves both terms together and leaves D near zero; a strategy transition
preserves competence while conflict agreement falls, producing large
positive D.

Maximum D over training, across all fifteen runs: C1 seed 3 reaches
0.79; the next most extreme run reaches 0.29; eleven of fifteen remain
below 0.06.

The C1 seed 3 trajectory of conflict utility-agreement is:

```
0.54 0.49 0.59 0.75 0.78 0.73 0.66 0.62 0.76 0.84 0.93 0.96 0.98
0.73 0.61 0.60 0.52 0.45 0.84 0.23 0.21
```

The run acquires behavior consistent with the utility rule to 0.98 at
60% of training, declines through the second half, recovers transiently
to 0.84 at 90%, and finishes at 0.21, aligned with the wording rule
(conflict cue-agreement 0.785; cue-only accuracy 1.00). In-distribution
accuracy is 0.99–1.00 from 65% of training onward.

Three observations constrain interpretation. The record is internally
consistent: conflict agreement with the two rules is complementary by
construction, and the identity holds exactly at all 21 checkpoints.
In-distribution accuracy is preserved and improving throughout the
decline, so global loss of task competence does not account for it. The
transition occurs during the interval in which C1 presents choice
examples (45–90%), so absence of practice does not account for it
either. The paired C2 and C3 runs from identical initial parameters
finish at 0.960 and 0.993.

This observation does not establish that structure-first ordering causes
instability: the event occurs in one of five C1 runs and zero of ten
C2 and C3 runs, which does not distinguish a curriculum-dependent
phenomenon from stochastic variation. It establishes something narrower.
The checkpointed record identifies temporal structure — acquisition,
maintenance, decline, transient recovery, and transition toward the
alternative rule — that final-checkpoint evaluation could not observe.
The finding motivates a hypothesis about strategy stability; it does not
test one.

## 6. Exploratory internal analyses

The following analyses were performed after the primary experiment, on
two completed runs (C2 and C3, seed 0), and are post-hoc and
hypothesis-generating. Results are summarized in Figure 4.

### 6.1 Preference-class information is linearly recoverable across held-out agents

Linear probes with shuffled-label controls recover the agent's λ class
from residual-stream activations at the agent token. Because λ is fixed
per agent, probe accuracy could reflect agent identity alone. We
therefore split by agent: of ten agents, five are used for probe
training and five held out, alternating within each λ class so that both
classes appear on both sides, with all examples involving held-out
agents excluded from probe training. Selectivity on held-out agents
remains high (0.77 at layer 2 in C2; 1.00 at layer 3 in C3).

This supports the presence of preference-class information beyond
memorization of the probed identities. It is a single agent-level split
with ten agents; variance across partitions is unmeasured. It does not
establish that the model uses this information to select an alternative.

A developmental map of probe selectivity across layers and checkpoints
shows nonzero selectivity (approximately 0.39) at initialization, before
any training. This baseline is a property of the shared-agent evaluation
rather than of a learned representation, and it illustrates why probe
values require controls and a reference level.

### 6.2 Steering produces a controlled behavioral effect

At probe-nominated layers, adding a scaled class-mean difference vector
to the residual stream changes conflict behavior monotonically, with
dose-response spreads of 0.18 (C2, layer 2) and 0.14 (C3, layer 3).
Matched controls — the same intervention at a non-nominated layer, and a
norm-matched random direction at the nominated layer — produce spreads
of at most 0.05. The direction of the effect was stated before the runs.

Under these interventions the preference-associated state has causal
influence on conflict behavior. This does not establish that the
direction is sufficient to implement the strategy or that it constitutes
the mechanism.

### 6.3 Ablation does not support unique localization

Projecting the preference-associated direction out of the residual
stream at the nominated layer reduces utility-side conflict behavior by
0.11 (C2) and 0.17 (C3); norm-matched random directions produce
approximately no change. However, in C2 the same projection at a
control layer produces a comparable reduction (0.13).

The evidence supports partial causal dependence on the
preference-associated direction under the tested ablation, and does not
support unique localization at the nominated layer. Because the behavior
is impaired rather than eliminated, we do not describe the direction as
necessary.

### 6.4 Cross-run activation patching does not transfer strategy

We tested whether substituting one run's residual state into the other
transfers conflict behavior toward the donor. An initial aggregate
metric — the recipient's shift toward the donor's accuracy, normalized
by the donor–recipient difference — appeared to indicate transfer. That
metric is unstable when donor and recipient accuracies are close, which
was the case at the final checkpoint, and it was retracted.

Per-example auditing on the items where the two runs disagree shows
candidate-layer patches siding with the donor on 16–51% of such items,
at or below the mismatched-donor control in one cell, while late-layer
patches side with the donor on 98–100% because the decision is already
determined at that depth. We therefore do not find support for the
hypothesis that the tested residual state is a portable carrier of the
run's strategy tendency.

Taken together, these analyses constrain rather than complete a
mechanistic account: preference-associated information is recoverable
and causally influential, but the evidence does not support a uniquely
localized or transferable implementation.

## 7. Four structures that must be distinguished

The environment makes explicit a distinction that is easy to lose in
practice. There is no single graph of what a model knows; there are at
least four structures, and in this experiment they do not agree.

The **generating structure** is what produced the data: λ and payoffs
determine utility, which determines the choice, after which wording is
assigned. The wording variable is downstream of the label, not upstream
of it.

The **observational structure** is what predicts labels in the corpus.
Here both utility and wording × scene are perfect predictors during
training, so the corpus supports an edge from wording to choice that
does not exist in the generating structure.

The **developmental structure** concerns which training experiences
affect the acquisition, retention, or stability of others, and must be
inferred from curriculum interventions and trajectories rather than from
the corpus.

The **mechanistic structure** concerns which internal representations
and computations causally contribute to behavior, and requires internal
measurement and intervention.

Collapsing these into one notion of "relationship" conflates corpus
correlation, data-generating causality, developmental dependence, and
learned computation. This experiment separates them by construction, and
we maintain the separation in all reported artifacts.

## 8. Discussion

The preregistered hypothesis was that changing the order of otherwise
identical experience would select between competing strategies. The
primary experiment does not support it: fourteen of fifteen runs
converged toward the utility strategy regardless of ordering, and paired
differences showed no consistent direction.

Preserving the training trajectory nonetheless produced two results that
endpoint evaluation could not. The first is a large, fully reproducible
effect of ordering on mid-training conflict behavior, whose most likely
explanation is competence degradation from absence of recent practice
rather than strategy change. The second is a single run in which
conflict behavior and in-distribution competence dissociate strongly and
persistently, which is not explained by degradation or by absence of
practice.

This distinction suggests that strategy selection and strategy stability
may be separable phenomena, and that the outcome variable chosen for the
primary criterion — final strategy preference — was likely not the most
informative one available. The appropriate next questions concern when a
strategy becomes established, how stable it is afterward, what
experiences destabilize it, and whether a curriculum intervention can
predictably alter transition rates.

The internal analyses reinforce the same methodological point at a
different level. Probe recoverability alone would have supported a
representational account; steering strengthens the causal claim;
ablation complicates localization; patching rejects a transfer
hypothesis that the aggregate statistic initially appeared to support.
At each stage, additional evidence changed the account rather than
confirming it.

## 9. Follow-up experiment

The transition described in §5.2 was observed after the primary
experiment and cannot serve as confirmatory evidence. A follow-up
experiment, preregistered before any new training, will test whether
curriculum composition affects the probability, timing, or persistence
of transitions between competing strategies after initial acquisition.

Commitment, transition, and reversal will be defined operationally
before data collection, with a persistence requirement across adjacent
checkpoints and an in-distribution competence gate so that degradation
is not classified as strategy change. Because the initial thresholds
were selected with the observed trajectory visible, they are declared
exploratory, will be frozen prior to the new runs, and the primary
analysis will report sensitivity across a pre-declared neighboring
grid. The primary outcome will be the probability of leaving a committed
state; the design must separate curriculum-specific instability from
seed-level stochasticity, interference from the immediately preceding
phase, tail composition effects, and optimization instability unrelated
to strategy.

The criterion of interest is prospective: whether a relationship
inferred from the present trajectories predicts a difference in
stability dynamics in newly trained runs under preregistered conditions.
A confirmed prediction strengthens the inferred relationship; a
disconfirmed prediction weakens it.

## 10. Limitations

The study covers one synthetic environment at one cue-complexity level,
one architecture at 10.9M parameters, a single-pass training regime, and
five paired seeds per condition. The preregistered decision rule is not
an equivalence test, so failure to detect a consistent effect of
curriculum order is not evidence that no such effect exists.

The reproducible mid-training effect (§5.1) has a straightforward
candidate explanation that we do not attempt to adjudicate further here.
The strategy transition (§5.2) occurred once. All internal analyses
(§6) are post-hoc, drawn from two runs of a single seed, and
unreplicated; the probe generalization result rests on one agent-level
split. Probe results establish recoverability under a specific probe
family and evaluation scheme, and intervention results establish effects
under specific interventions rather than complete causal descriptions.

The synthetic environment provides strong experimental control at the
cost of external validity. Nothing reported here establishes analogous
dynamics in large pretrained language models.

## 11. Conclusion

We constructed a controlled training environment in which two predictive
strategies were observationally indistinguishable on the training
distribution and tested whether curriculum order altered final strategy
preference. The preregistered endpoint criterion was not met. Checkpoint
analysis identified a large, reproducible mid-training effect of
ordering that is consistent with competence degradation rather than
strategy change, and one late strategy transition that was not
attributable to loss of ordinary task competence, motivating a follow-up
study of post-acquisition strategy stability. Exploratory probe and
intervention analyses provided evidence for a preference-associated
internal representation with causal influence on behavior, while failing
to support unique localization or reliable cross-run state transfer.
These findings support the use of developmental trajectories and
controlled interventions to distinguish endpoint behavior from the
processes that produce it.

## Availability and reproducibility

The repository contains the environment generator with machine-verified
equivalence constraints and invariant tests, the preflight harness, the
append-only preregistration including the failed calibration gates and
the contradicted directional hypothesis, all training, scoring, and
analysis code, the figure-generation script, and every evidence record
with its provenance chain (run identifier, checkpoint, diagnostic set,
commit). A browser-based analysis interface serves the same artifacts
for inspection and is intended as a reproducibility tool rather than a
contribution of this paper. An incident log documents two process
failures and their resolution: a results table produced by an AI
collaborator that was confabulated and detected before it entered any
artifact or analysis, and the unstable transfer metric described in
§6.4. Total compute for all reported results is approximately ten
GPU-hours on a single NVIDIA L4.
