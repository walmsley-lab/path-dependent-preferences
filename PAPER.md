## Abstract

Training accuracy cannot distinguish between strategies that make
identical predictions on the training distribution. We construct a
synthetic social-choice environment in which this ambiguity is exact:
choices are generated by a latent utility rule, while a second predictor
based on wording and scene context is constructed to agree with that
rule on every training example and disagree on held-out conflict items.
We test whether presentation order affects which strategy governs
behavior by training fifteen 10.9M-parameter decoder-only Transformers
under three orderings of an identical example multiset, with five paired
initialization seeds per condition and 21 checkpoints per run. The
preregistered endpoint criterion was not met: fourteen of fifteen runs
finished on the utility side of the conflict diagnostic, with no
consistent direction in paired curriculum differences. Checkpoint
analysis nevertheless revealed dynamics obscured by endpoint evaluation.
All five choices-first runs showed a transient decline in conflict
utility agreement when choice examples were removed, accompanied by a
similar decline in in-distribution accuracy. More importantly, three of
fifteen runs showed post-acquisition declines in utility agreement while
retaining in-distribution competence; in one run, utility agreement fell
from 0.98 to 0.21 while in-distribution accuracy remained 0.99–1.00.
These observations are post hoc, and differences in their frequency
across curricula are not informative at this sample size. Exploratory
mechanistic analyses identify preference-class information that
generalizes across held-out agents and influences behavior under
steering, while ablation and activation-patching controls do not support
unique layer localization or reliable cross-run state transfer.
Together, these results do not establish an effect of curriculum order
on strategy selection or stability, but show that checkpointed
evaluation can distinguish transient competence loss from changes in
strategy-consistent behavior that endpoint evaluation would obscure.
They motivate a preregistered follow-up testing whether curriculum
composition affects post-acquisition strategy stability.

## 1. Introduction

A model can produce correct predictions for more than one reason. When a
training distribution contains several regularities that imply the same
labels, successful optimization does not identify which regularity a
model has learned. Shortcut learning, spurious correlation, and
underspecification are variants of this problem.

The problem is acute when behavior is read as evidence of a stable
disposition. A system that consistently selects outcomes favorable to
another party may have learned to represent and compare outcomes, or may
have learned surface features that correlate with those outcomes in its
training environment. The two are behaviorally indistinguishable until
the regularities are placed in conflict.

A second question follows. When two models receive identical
information, does the order of presentation affect which predictive
strategy *governs their behavior*? Curriculum learning typically asks
whether ordering changes performance or sample efficiency. We ask
whether it changes the strategy supporting otherwise similar
performance. The distinction between acquiring information and using it
to determine behavior matters here and recurs throughout: a model may
represent quantities relevant to both strategies while its outputs are
governed largely by one.

Answering either question requires separating four things that are
easily conflated. The **generating structure** is the process that
produced the data. The **observational structure** is the set of
relationships that predict labels within the resulting corpus; it need
not match the generating structure, and in our environment it
deliberately does not. The **developmental structure** concerns which
training experiences affect the acquisition or retention of others, and
can only be inferred from interventions on training itself. The
**mechanistic structure** concerns which internal representations
causally contribute to behavior, and requires internal measurement. We
return to this distinction in §7; it motivates the environment design
below, and keeping the four separate is what allows a planted
correlation to be studied without assuming it is either causal in the
world or implemented in the model.

Studying this in a large pretrained model would confound the variables
of interest: its training distribution, ordering, and internal
representations are not fully controllable or observable. We therefore
construct a small environment in which the generating process is known
and two strategies can be made exactly observationally equivalent during
training.

The primary question is: **when identical training examples support two
equally predictive rules, does changing only their order change which
rule governs a Transformer's final behavior?** Because we preserve
checkpoints throughout training, we can also ask a second question that
becomes relevant given the primary result: **does endpoint behavior
describe the path by which a model reached it?** Throughout, we use
*developmental trajectory* to refer specifically to the sequence of
measurements across checkpoints during a single training run.

## 2. Environment

### 2.1 Generating rule

The environment contains ten recurring named agents. Each is assigned a
latent preference parameter λ ∈ {0.2, 0.8}, the weight placed on the
agent's own outcome relative to a partner's. Assignment is deterministic
per seed and constrained so that each λ class contains at least two
names of each sex association, preventing λ from being predictable from
that surface property of the name.

Each scenario presents two alternatives with integer payoff changes for
the agent and the partner. Utility is U(o) = λ·d_self(o) +
(1−λ)·d_other(o), and the generated label is y = argmax_o U(o).
Utilities are computed in integer fifths so that exact ties can be
constructed for diagnostic use. The parameter λ is never stated in the
text presented to the model.

### 2.2 Constructed observational equivalence

After the utility rule determines the label, the generator assigns verb
phrases to the two alternatives. Phrases belong to semantic classes
(cooperative or self-interested, four surface forms each), and the
mapping from class to chosen alternative is conditioned on scene
("market" or "river"). The corpus therefore contains a second rule,
wording × scene → choice, constructed to predict the same label as the
utility rule on every training example. This constraint is verified
mechanically by the generator's invariant tests.

Either strategy achieves perfect training accuracy. The identification
problem is thus present by construction rather than by accident, and its
severity is known exactly (Figure 1).

### 2.3 Diagnostic distributions

Three held-out evaluations separate the strategies. **Conflict** items
assign wording so that the two rules predict opposite alternatives.
**No-cue** items use neutral verb phrases, leaving the wording rule
undefined. **Cue-only** items use exact utility ties, leaving the
utility rule undefined. A fourth in-distribution set measures ordinary
task competence.

Conflict-set agreement with the utility rule is the primary behavioral
measure. In-distribution accuracy measures competence and cannot
identify strategy.

## 3. Design

### 3.1 Models and training

We train six-layer decoder-only Transformers with hidden dimension 384,
six attention heads, and 10,924,032 parameters, using a next-token
prediction objective. Within each seed, the three curriculum conditions
use the same vocabulary and the same model initialization, both verified
by hash; only the presentation order of training examples differs.

Each seed's corpus contains 2.4 million examples (approximately 89
million tokens), divided equally between two types. **Structure
examples** describe payoff relations using neutral wording and provide
no evidence about any agent's λ. **Choice examples** present agent
decisions; they are the only examples from which agent-specific
preferences can be inferred, and they carry the constructed wording
correlation of §2.2. The curriculum intervention (§3.2) changes the
ordering of these two types without changing their contents or
frequencies.

Corpus scale was set by calibration (§3.3) rather than by convenience.
In a diagnostic run of 6,839 steps, held-out agreement with the utility
rule crosses 80% at approximately 2,400 steps and then plateaus at
0.80–0.86 from roughly half that trajectory onward. The single-pass
design places the endpoint at 4,459 steps, about 65% of the diagnostic
trajectory and inside the plateau, so the primary measurement is not
taken at a threshold graze.

Examples are packed into 320-token blocks that do not cross curriculum
phase boundaries, and trained for a single pass with batch size 64,
yielding 285,374 blocks and 4,459 optimizer steps. Optimization uses
AdamW (learning rate 3×10⁻⁴, weight decay 0.1, gradient-norm clipping at
1.0) with a 200-step linear warmup followed by a **flat** learning rate,
and no dropout, in full precision. The flat schedule is deliberate: under
a decaying schedule, examples appearing later in training would receive
systematically smaller updates, which is itself an ordering effect and
would confound the manipulation under test.

Structure examples are deduplicated by exact string and choice examples
by scenario key across surface templates, with rejection counts recorded
in the corpus manifest. Each seed's training examples are therefore
unique, so a single pass is a developmental trajectory over distinct
material rather than repeated exposure to the same items. Corpus
constraints, deduplication rules, full training configurations, library
versions, and hardware are recorded in the run manifests accompanying
every checkpoint.

### 3.2 Curriculum intervention

The independent variable is presentation order. Three curricula contain
the same example multiset and an identical final 10% tail (Figure 2):

- **C1**: structure (45%) → choices (45%) → tail (10%)
- **C2**: choices (45%) → structure (45%) → tail (10%)
- **C3**: interleaved (90%) → tail (10%)

The intervention therefore changes when information is encountered, not
what information is available.

Five seeds are used per condition. Within a seed index, all three
curricula begin from identical initial parameters and use an identical
vocabulary, both verified by hash, which controls initialization across
the paired comparison.

We state the comparison precisely, because it is the paper's central
methodological claim. Within a paired seed, **the order of lines in the
curriculum file is the only intentionally varied factor.** The example
multiset, vocabulary, initial parameters, random seed, optimizer and its
hyperparameters, block size, batch size, step count (2,400,000 lines →
285,374 blocks → 4,459 steps in every condition), and final 10% tail are
identical. Training performs no shuffling of its own — the curriculum
file fixes the sequence — and the model contains no dropout, so a run is
deterministic given its seed and curriculum file. Consequently, paired
runs diverge only through the effect of ordering on the optimization
path, rather than through any additional stochastic difference. Optimizer
moments and RNG state are checkpointed at phase boundaries for planned
transfer experiments. A preflight check refuses to launch any run
violating multiset equality, tail identity, or initialization pairing.
Twenty-one checkpoints are preserved per run, giving 15 × 21 = 315
evaluated checkpoints.

### 3.3 Calibration, preregistration, and their chronology

The order of decisions matters for interpretation, so we state it
explicitly. Pilot runs were used to calibrate training duration and to
specify the primary analysis. **These pilot runs are excluded from the
confirmatory battery reported here.** An initial directional hypothesis
— that structure-first training would favor the utility strategy — was
specified before the pilots and was contradicted by them. Before
training the five-seed battery, we therefore preregistered a
direction-agnostic endpoint criterion. The preregistration was frozen
before any confirmatory run was trained, and no outcome from those runs
was observed before freezing.

Calibration also established the acquisition dynamics used to size the
corpus (§3.1): the wording rule shows a partial-memorization phase
followed by a sharp transition to near-ceiling accuracy, while the
utility rule rises gradually with a persistent generalization gap and a
lower ceiling. Two earlier calibration gates failed at chance and were
traced to undertrained pilots; the corpus was then rescaled from
measured acquisition curves. The full calibration history, including
both failures, is in the repository's preregistration record.

Writing A_C,s for final-checkpoint conflict utility agreement in
condition C and seed s, and Δ_s = A_{C1,s} − A_{C2,s}, the preregistered
criterion required

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;sign(Δ_s) = c for all s (some common c ∈ {+, −}),
and&nbsp;&nbsp; (1/5) Σ_s |Δ_s| > 0.10.

This rule was prespecified but is not a conventional population-level
hypothesis test. Unanimous sign agreement is sensitive to differences
near zero, and a mean of absolute differences ignores direction and can
be dominated by a single extreme pair. We report the criterion exactly
as preregistered and do not use it in subsequent experiments; the
follow-up (§9) specifies an estimand, a power analysis, an
interim-analysis plan, and multiplicity control.

## 4. Result: no consistent endpoint effect of training order

Conflict-set utility agreement at the final checkpoint:

| Seed | C1 | C2 | C3 | C1 − C2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.783 | 0.815 | 0.783 | −0.033 |
| 1 | 0.990 | 0.940 | 0.955 | +0.050 |
| 2 | 0.978 | 0.953 | 0.735 | +0.025 |
| 3 | 0.215 | 0.960 | 0.993 | −0.745 |
| 4 | 0.980 | 0.983 | 0.993 | −0.003 |

The paired differences have mixed signs, so the preregistered criterion
is not met. The mean absolute difference (0.171) exceeds the threshold
but is produced entirely by seed 3.

Fourteen of fifteen runs finish on the utility side of the conflict
diagnostic (0.735–0.993) under all three orderings. Under the tested
environment, cue complexity, model scale, training budget, and
curriculum manipulation, we find no consistent evidence that training
order determines final strategy preference. The design does not test
equivalence, so this does not establish that curriculum order cannot
affect strategy selection.

Viewed only at the final checkpoint, the extreme value (C1, seed 3) would
reasonably be treated as an anomalous run, and the moderate values
(C1 seed 0 at 0.783, C3 seed 2 at 0.735) as unremarkable variation.

## 5. Result: the checkpointed record

Figure 3 shows conflict utility-agreement across all 21 checkpoints for
all fifteen runs. The analyses in this section were not preregistered
and are exploratory.

One finding here is methodological and shapes the rest of the paper.
Conflict-set agreement was chosen as the primary measure because it
separates the two strategies, but it is not a pure measure of strategy
use: a run that has lost general task competence will also fall away
from the utility rule. Observed conflict agreement therefore reflects
both task competence and strategy use, and the two must be separated
before either can be read from it. Section 5.1 describes a large effect
attributable mainly to the first term, and §5.2 isolates cases
attributable to the second by conditioning on the first. This is a
result of the experiment rather than a repair to it: identifying which
strategy governs behavior requires conditioning on whether the model can
still perform the underlying task.

### 5.1 A reproducible mid-training effect of ordering

All five C2 runs rise to approximately 0.97 utility-agreement by 50% of
training, decline to a condition mean of 0.74 immediately after 45% —
the point at which the C2 curriculum stops presenting choice examples —
remain at that level throughout the structure phase, and return to a
mean of 0.93 within the shared tail that reintroduces choice examples at
90%. The pattern appears in five of five C2 runs and is absent in C1 and
C3, whose choice examples continue through the corresponding interval.

As a statement about developmental trajectories, curriculum ordering has
a large and fully reproducible effect in this environment. The endpoint
comparison in §4 cannot detect it because C2 recovers before the final
checkpoint.

The most likely explanation is not strategy change. Across the C2
decline, conflict agreement and in-distribution accuracy move together
closely (at 60%: 0.76 and 0.78; at 70%: 0.74 and 0.74; at 95%: 0.94 and
0.95). This co-movement is consistent with degradation of choice-task
competence in the absence of choice examples, and its recovery when they
return, rather than with a transition between strategies.

### 5.2 Post-commitment declines with preserved competence

Because degradation and strategy change are confusable at the level of
conflict accuracy alone, we separate them with a competence-gated
definition. A run is *committed* when conflict utility-agreement is at
least 0.90 for three consecutive checkpoints; a *transition* occurs when
agreement subsequently falls below 0.80 for three consecutive
checkpoints while in-distribution accuracy remains at or above 0.95.
The competence gate ensures that a run losing general task ability is
not classified as changing strategy.

Three of fifteen runs meet this definition: C1 seeds 0 and 3, and C3
seed 2 (C1 2/5, C2 0/5, C3 1/5). The classification is unchanged across
the pre-declared sensitivity grid (commitment threshold 0.85 or 0.90,
persistence 2 or 3 checkpoints). We note that these thresholds were
chosen with the trajectories visible and treat the definition as
exploratory in origin; it is frozen for the follow-up (§9).

The three runs differ in severity. C1 seed 0 rises to 0.97, declines to
0.71 at 85% of training, and finishes at 0.78, with in-distribution
accuracy reaching 1.00. C3 seed 2 rises to 0.97 and erodes gradually to
0.73, with accuracy at 1.00 from 70% onward. C1 seed 3 is the only full
crossing: its trajectory of conflict utility-agreement is

```
0.54 0.49 0.59 0.75 0.78 0.73 0.66 0.62 0.76 0.84 0.93 0.96 0.98
0.73 0.61 0.60 0.52 0.45 0.84 0.23 0.21
```

reaching 0.98 at 60% of training, declining through the second half,
recovering transiently to 0.84 at 90%, and finishing at 0.21 — aligned
with the wording rule (conflict cue-agreement 0.785; cue-only accuracy
1.00) — while in-distribution accuracy is 0.99–1.00 from 65% onward. A
complementary summary statistic, D = (in-distribution accuracy −
conflict utility-agreement), separates the run further: its maximum over
training is 0.79 for C1 seed 3, against 0.29 for the next most extreme
run, with eleven of fifteen runs below 0.06.

Three observations constrain interpretation. The records are internally
consistent: conflict agreement with the two rules is complementary by
construction, and the identity holds exactly at all 21 checkpoints of
every run. In-distribution accuracy is preserved or improving
throughout each decline, so loss of general task competence does not
account for them. In C1, the declines occur during the interval in which
choice examples are presented (45–90%), so absence of practice does not
account for them either; this distinguishes them from the C2 effect in
§5.1. For C1 seed 3, the paired C2 and C3 runs from identical initial
parameters finish at 0.960 and 0.993.

These observations do not establish that curriculum order affects
transition rates. No test of transition rate was preregistered — the
definition itself is post-hoc — so we report the contrast descriptively:
two of five C1 runs and zero of five C2 runs meet the definition, a
difference that a Fisher exact calculation places well inside what
sampling alone would produce at this size (p = 0.44). We report the
value to forestall over-reading the counts, not as a hypothesis test.
What the checkpointed record establishes is that post-commitment
decline occurs at a non-trivial base rate — three of fifteen runs — with
graded severity, and that it is separable from competence degradation by
a definition that is stable across thresholds. Final-checkpoint
evaluation would have recorded one extreme value and two moderate ones,
without the information that all three follow acquisition of the
strategy they later lose.

## 6. Exploratory internal analyses

The following analyses were performed after the primary experiment, on
two completed runs (C2 and C3, seed 0), and are post-hoc and
hypothesis-generating. Results are summarized in Figure 4.

### 6.1 Preference-class information is linearly recoverable across held-out agents

Linear probes with shuffled-label controls recover the agent's λ class
from residual-stream activations at the agent token. Because λ is fixed
per agent, probe accuracy could reflect agent identity alone. We
therefore split by agent: of ten agents, five are used for probe
training and five held out, alternating within each λ class so that both
classes appear on both sides, with all examples involving held-out
agents excluded from probe training. Selectivity on held-out agents
remains high (0.77 at layer 2 in C2; 1.00 at layer 3 in C3).

This supports the presence of preference-class information beyond
memorization of the probed identities. It is a single agent-level split
with ten agents; variance across partitions is unmeasured. It does not
establish that the model uses this information to select an alternative.

A developmental map of probe selectivity across layers and checkpoints
shows nonzero selectivity (approximately 0.39) at initialization, before
any training. This baseline is a property of the shared-agent evaluation
rather than of a learned representation, and it illustrates why probe
values require controls and a reference level.

### 6.2 Steering produces a controlled behavioral effect

At probe-nominated layers, adding a scaled class-mean difference vector
to the residual stream changes conflict behavior monotonically, with
dose-response spreads of 0.18 (C2, layer 2) and 0.14 (C3, layer 3).
Matched controls — the same intervention at a non-nominated layer, and a
norm-matched random direction at the nominated layer — produce spreads
of at most 0.05. The direction of the effect was stated before the runs.

Under these interventions the preference-associated state has causal
influence on conflict behavior. This does not establish that the
direction is sufficient to implement the strategy or that it constitutes
the mechanism.

### 6.3 Ablation does not support unique localization

Projecting the preference-associated direction out of the residual
stream at the nominated layer reduces utility-side conflict behavior by
0.11 (C2) and 0.17 (C3); norm-matched random directions produce
approximately no change. However, in C2 the same projection at a
control layer produces a comparable reduction (0.13).

The evidence supports partial causal dependence on the
preference-associated direction under the tested ablation, and does not
support unique localization at the nominated layer. Because the behavior
is impaired rather than eliminated, we do not describe the direction as
necessary.

### 6.4 Cross-run activation patching does not transfer strategy

We tested whether substituting one run's residual state into the other
transfers conflict behavior toward the donor. An initial aggregate
metric — the recipient's shift toward the donor's accuracy, normalized
by the donor–recipient difference — appeared to indicate transfer. That
metric is unstable when donor and recipient accuracies are close, which
was the case at the final checkpoint, and it was retracted.

Per-example auditing on the items where the two runs disagree shows
candidate-layer patches siding with the donor on 16–51% of such items,
at or below the mismatched-donor control in one cell, while late-layer
patches side with the donor on 98–100% because the decision is already
determined at that depth. We therefore do not find support for the
hypothesis that the tested residual state is a portable carrier of the
run's strategy tendency.

Taken together, these analyses constrain rather than complete a
mechanistic account: preference-associated information is recoverable
and causally influential, but the evidence does not support a uniquely
localized or transferable implementation.

## 7. The four structures, revisited

The distinction introduced in §1 is not merely terminological in this
environment; the four structures demonstrably disagree, and the
experiment shows where.

The **generating structure** is what produced the data: λ and payoffs
determine utility, which determines the choice, after which wording is
assigned. The wording variable is downstream of the label, not upstream
of it.

The **observational structure** is what predicts labels in the corpus.
Here both utility and wording × scene are perfect predictors during
training, so the corpus supports an edge from wording to choice that
does not exist in the generating structure.

The **developmental structure** concerns which training experiences
affect the acquisition, retention, or stability of others, and must be
inferred from curriculum interventions and trajectories rather than from
the corpus.

The **mechanistic structure** concerns which internal representations
and computations causally contribute to behavior, and requires internal
measurement and intervention.

Collapsing these into one notion of "relationship" conflates corpus
correlation, data-generating causality, developmental dependence, and
learned computation. This experiment separates them by construction, and
we maintain the separation in all reported artifacts.

## 8. Discussion

The preregistered hypothesis was that changing the order of otherwise
identical experience would select between competing strategies. The
primary experiment does not support it: fourteen of fifteen runs
converged toward the utility strategy regardless of ordering, and paired
differences showed no consistent direction.

Preserving the training trajectory nonetheless produced two results that
endpoint evaluation could not. The first is a large, fully reproducible
effect of ordering on mid-training conflict behavior, whose most likely
explanation is competence degradation from absence of recent practice
rather than strategy change. The second is a set of three runs in which conflict behavior declines
after commitment while in-distribution competence is preserved, one of
them proceeding to full alignment with the competing rule; these are not
explained by degradation or by absence of practice.

This distinction suggests that strategy selection and strategy stability
may be separable phenomena, and that the outcome variable chosen for the
primary criterion — final strategy preference — was likely not the most
informative one available. The appropriate next questions concern when a
strategy becomes established, how stable it is afterward, what
experiences destabilize it, and whether a curriculum intervention can
predictably alter transition rates.

The internal analyses reinforce the same methodological point at a
different level. Probe recoverability alone would have supported a
representational account; steering strengthens the causal claim;
ablation complicates localization; patching rejects a transfer
hypothesis that the aggregate statistic initially appeared to support.
At each stage, additional evidence changed the account rather than
confirming it.

## 9. Follow-up experiment

The declines described in §5.2 were identified after the primary
experiment and cannot serve as confirmatory evidence. A protocol is
frozen before any new training (`PREREG_PHASE_B.md`, summarized in
Appendix A) to test whether curriculum composition affects the
probability of post-acquisition strategy transitions.

The design is two arms (C1, C2) at n = 25 runs per arm, paired by
initialization within seed index, with the §5.2 definitions frozen and
their sensitivity grid pre-declared. The primary endpoint is transition
rate compared by Fisher's exact test; a secondary family (competence-
gated dissociation, time to transition, late-training transition
probability, drawdown, and final agreement) is reported under
Benjamini-Hochberg control. Simulation (6,000 trials, Fisher exact, one-sided α = 0.05) gives 82%
power at the base rate observed here (0.40 versus 0.05), 57% under
conservative assumptions (0.30 versus 0.05), and 19% under pessimistic
ones (0.25 versus 0.10). A null result at this sample size will be
reported as inconclusive for small effects rather than as evidence of no
difference. For reference, a design at 10 runs per arm — a natural first
instinct — would have 3% power at the observed rates, and we note this
because the calculation, not intuition, determined the sample size. One interim analysis is specified with alpha spending, for
efficacy only.

The protocol also records a prospective prediction — that the transition
rate under C1 will exceed that under C2 by at least 15 percentage points
— so that the follow-up tests a relationship inferred from the present
trajectories rather than describing them. Confirmation strengthens that
relationship; disconfirmation weakens it, and either outcome is reported.

## 10. Limitations

The study covers one synthetic environment at one cue-complexity level,
one architecture at 10.9M parameters, a single-pass training regime, and
five paired seeds per condition. The preregistered decision rule is not
an equivalence test, so failure to detect a consistent effect of
curriculum order is not evidence that no such effect exists.

The reproducible mid-training effect (§5.1) has a straightforward
candidate explanation that we do not attempt to adjudicate further here.
Post-commitment decline (§5.2) occurred in three runs, and the
between-condition contrast is uninformative at this sample size; the
operational definition was chosen with the trajectories visible. All internal analyses
(§6) are post-hoc, drawn from two runs of a single seed, and
unreplicated; the probe generalization result rests on one agent-level
split. Probe results establish recoverability under a specific probe
family and evaluation scheme, and intervention results establish effects
under specific interventions rather than complete causal descriptions.

The synthetic environment provides strong experimental control at the
cost of external validity. Nothing reported here establishes analogous
dynamics in large pretrained language models.

## 11. Conclusion

We constructed a controlled training environment in which two predictive
strategies were observationally indistinguishable on the training
distribution and tested whether curriculum order altered final strategy
preference. The preregistered endpoint criterion was not met. Checkpoint
analysis identified a large, reproducible mid-training effect of
ordering that is consistent with competence degradation rather than
strategy change, and post-commitment strategy declines in three of
fifteen runs that were not attributable to loss of ordinary task
competence, one of which crossed fully to the competing rule. These
motivate a preregistered follow-up on post-acquisition stability, for
which the present data supply a base rate but no evidence of a
between-condition difference. Exploratory probe and
intervention analyses provided evidence for a preference-associated
internal representation with causal influence on behavior, while failing
to support unique localization or reliable cross-run state transfer.
These results do not establish an effect of curriculum order on strategy
selection or stability. They do show that checkpointed evaluation can
distinguish transient competence loss from changes in
strategy-consistent behavior, a distinction that endpoint evaluation
cannot make and that determined both the interpretation of this
experiment and the design of the next.

## Availability and reproducibility

The repository contains the environment generator with machine-verified
equivalence constraints and invariant tests, the preflight harness, the
append-only preregistration including the failed calibration gates and
the contradicted directional hypothesis, all training, scoring, and
analysis code, the figure-generation script, and every evidence record
with its provenance chain (run identifier, checkpoint, diagnostic set,
commit). A browser-based analysis interface serves the same artifacts
for inspection and is intended as a reproducibility tool rather than a
contribution of this paper. An incident log documents two process
failures and their resolution: a results table produced by an AI
collaborator that was confabulated and detected before it entered any
artifact or analysis, and the unstable transfer metric described in
§6.4. Total compute for all reported results is approximately ten
GPU-hours on a single NVIDIA L4.
